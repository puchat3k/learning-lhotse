# Research collaborators wanted: When conversations lose the question

*Learning Lhotse | Frays.ai | Exploratory research invitation | November 2026*

A conversation can be coherent sentence by sentence while failing to answer the question that made it worth having. People and AI assistants can follow the latest turn, shift into a familiar professional frame, or generate persuasive explanations while leaving earlier questions, conditions and proposals unresolved.

At Frays.ai, we are exploring Learning Lhotse: a research programme examining semantic drift, conversational framing, context persistence and epistemic provenance across human–human and human–AI exchanges. Our objective is to study observable dialogue behaviour—not detect who used AI, infer hidden motives, or assign truthfulness scores to people.

## Questions we want to investigate

• Can we distinguish forgetting an earlier question, deliberately changing the frame, and legitimately challenging its premise?

• How do obligations and unanswered questions evolve across a direct 1+1 exchange, a four-message 2+2 window, and the complete conversation?

• Can a short, low-information response expose context persistence better than a detailed explanatory prompt?

• When a human reviews, edits or rejects AI-generated replies, does that improve semantic fidelity—or introduce new forms of drift?

## A bounded first study

We propose a source-preserving and reproducible evaluation: immutable transcripts; an explicit ledger of questions, offers and accepted or rejected framing changes; bidirectional annotation of dialogue acts; and independent blind judgments. We would compare last-pair-only, last-four-messages and full-history conditions against simple deterministic baselines. Genuine refusals and justified reframing count as valid responses, not errors.

Existing work on discourse intentions, Questions Under Discussion, common ground and conversational dialogue acts provides the starting point. We have an internal exploratory case study and provisional annotation protocol, not a validated benchmark. Any actual participant material requires appropriate authorization and privacy controls; synthetic cases are available as a safer starting point.

## Who we would like to hear from

Researchers and practitioners in computational linguistics, pragmatics, dialogue systems, human–AI interaction, evaluation science, negotiation and epistemic reasoning. Useful contributions could include annotation design, falsifiable hypotheses, controlled test sets, inter-rater studies, baseline implementations and independent methodological criticism.

This is an invitation to explore a study, not an announcement of funded academic positions, guaranteed compensation, or unrestricted access to private conversations. Collaboration terms and data use would be agreed separately.
